01

On Mill Dust

There is a residue that no sieve can refuse. It settles into the seams of the floorboards, into the cuffs of every apron, into the hair of the weigher, and it rides the air for years before anyone bothers to call it anything at all. We call it dust. Dust, however, is a lazy word, and this issue is not.

The plate at left is not a photograph of dust. It is dust: a lattice of random heights, sampled bilinearly and printed as ink dots the way a newsroom would. The orange flecks are where the lattice runs hot — the stones that will not cool.

Every mill keeps two ledgers. The first records what came in — the tonnes, the lots, the moisture, the river the boat came up from. The second records what came out. The two never agree, and the gap between them is the dust: a tenth of a gram per kilo, fine as breath, that no scale is willing to own.

Dust is the mill's shadow arithmetic. It tells you which stones ran hot, which nights the weather turned, which sack was torn at the dock and mended with wire. A foreman can read a season from a single scoop of it the way a sailor reads a sky — and he will be right more often than the ledgers admit.

The old houses of the mill town were built with dust in the mortar. Run a finger along the wall of the weigh house on Quay Street and you are reading, letter by letter, every winter the works stayed open. The city grew around the residue the way the residue grew around the grain.

The Sieve

Between the stone and the flour stands a machine that does almost nothing on purpose: the sieve. Twelve meshes of woven wire, shaken at a rhythm the ear can predict and the hand cannot. What passes is product. What remains is residue — and residue is where the story keeps its receipts.

The invisible tenth — weighed only by absence04 · 200 MESH · 0.07 MM

Sifting is the only act in the mill that admits it is losing. Every pass returns less than the last, and the difference is not waste but witness: the exact measure of what the grain could not say to the stone. The sifter's table is the one place in the works where the residue is allowed to speak.

The dust of the 200-mesh is what the town calls the white weather. In spring it stands up the river like a fog that will not lift, and the boats slow to a crawl through it, and the gulls carry it home, and it is on the breakfast plate before anyone remembers that it was ever a field.
